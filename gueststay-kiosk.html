Turning a kiosk pilot into a product hotels actually buy

The GuestStay kiosk proved guests would check themselves in. But it was slower than the front desk, hid useful steps and made no money. I redesigned it into a white-label, accessible product that cuts queues and sells upgrades.

The problem

The pilot did its job: it validated that guests wanted self check-in and that a kiosk could talk to the property management system. Hotels weren't convinced though. Guests had to re-enter details they'd already given, optional steps like card pre-authorisation and key cutting were hidden, there were no upsells, and staff still got pulled in to help.

Hoteliers were asking a fair question: why pay for a kiosk that does the front desk's job, but slower? My brief was to turn a validated prototype into a scalable product that cuts waits, makes money and flexes to each hotel's brand.

What I found

I audited the full guest journey across booking, pre-arrival, arrival and departure, and interviewed hoteliers and staff. Three things stood out:

  • Expectation mismatch. Pre-arrival emails promised a faster check-in, then guests were asked for the same info again at the kiosk.
  • Accessibility gaps. Screens were often mounted high and the on-screen keyboard took a third of the screen. Hard for kids, wheelchair users and shorter guests.
  • Operational drag. Staff were troubleshooting kiosks instead of hosting, and hoteliers had no data to justify the investment.

Competitor kiosks mostly just digitised the paper form. Very few did upsells, branding or accessibility well, so there was a clear gap.

Key decisions

Cut check-in from 8 to 10 screens down to 4 steps

Merged reservation lookup and review, consolidated guest details into one screen with inline validation, and folded pre-authorisation, key cutting and upsells into the main flow instead of side flows.

Treat upsells as part of the flow, not an ad

Room upgrades, late check-out and breakfast appear as modular, skippable steps at the moment they're relevant. This is what turned the kiosk from a cost into a revenue line for hotels.

Design for every height and ability

A fixed screen structure (header, context, main, actions, keyboard) so guests never switch input mode unexpectedly, plus large targets and WCAG-compliant contrast and type.

Go responsive and white-label instead of one tablet model

The pilot was tied to a single device. I moved it to a responsive layout across kiosk, tablet, mobile and desktop, with configurable logo, colour and type so each hotel gets its own branded kiosk.

Fix the words, not just the screens

Replaced vague prompts like "Check in now!" with honest, brandable templates such as "Complete your details in 3 minutes to skip the queue".

In the real world

At Point A Hotels in the UK, kiosks captured 3,000 guest emails in the first 90 days, data that would otherwise have stayed with OTAs. At the Classik Hotel Collection in Berlin, the first German hotel to adopt them, 100% of guests were using the kiosk voluntarily within four weeks, and the group ordered more for other properties.

Across Guestline in 2024 there were 120,000 kiosk interactions, around 6,000 staff hours saved and 2.6% incremental revenue from upsells (HospitalityNet).

What I'd take from it

The kiosk only became valuable once it stopped being a standalone tool. It now shares one design system with the Portal and the booking engine, so a guest gets the same experience from booking to check-out, and hotels get one product to configure. Next up is personalisation, like offering late check-out when a guest's flight is delayed.
